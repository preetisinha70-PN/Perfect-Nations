Suriname now owes in the currency it earns

Suriname has turned a US$150 million foreign-currency loan from the Inter-American Development Bank into a fixed-rate loan in its own currency. The debt is no smaller, but a devaluation can no longer make it larger.

  • $150mIDB loan converted from a floating rate into fixed-rate Surinamese dollars
  • 18 yrsnon-deliverable cross-currency swap provided by TCX to the IDB
  • 2044the year to which debt service is shielded from currency and rate swings
  • Firstexternal debt conversion into Surinamese dollars, and the first currency conversion in nearly three decades

On 8 October 2026 the Inter-American Development Bank announced that Suriname had converted a US$150 million IDB loan, carried at a floating rate in foreign currency, into a fixed-rate loan denominated in Surinamese dollars. It is the nation’s first conversion of external debt into its own currency, and, in the words of its Debt Management Office and its Minister of Finance and Planning, its first currency conversion in nearly three decades.

What it is

The instrument is not new money. It is a change to old money. Under the IDB’s Flexible Financing Facility, a borrower can change a loan’s currency or interest-rate basis after approval, using options already written into its loan agreement. Suriname used both. To make it possible, TCX provided the IDB with an 18-year non-deliverable cross-currency swap, which keeps the loan effectively in Surinamese dollars and shields debt service through 2044. The Bank notes that Suriname’s own market lacks the long-term local-currency benchmarks and hedges that private lenders would need to offer the same cover.

What it changes

The principal does not fall. The volatility does. A government whose budget runs in Surinamese dollars now owes this loan in Surinamese dollars, at a fixed rate. A devaluation no longer raises the cost of the debt overnight, and a rise in floating interest rates no longer reaches the budget. Two facts are not yet public: what the original loan financed, and the fixed rate it now carries. Both belong in the next statement from the Ministry of Finance and Planning.

Which standards it moves

None directly. It protects the budget that pays for all of them. The seven basic living standards are food, water, shelter, energy, education, health and livelihood. When debt service is owed in a foreign currency, a weaker national currency raises its cost before any of the seven is funded. Predictable debt service is the condition under which a ministry can commit to a decade of water mains or classrooms.

Where it could work next

IDB borrowers hold the same options in their loan agreements, by the Bank’s own account. Honduras is a candidate. On 1 October 2026 the IDB announced US$70 million for drinking water in the Central District of Tegucigalpa and Comayagüela, of which US$42 million comes from the Bank’s Ordinary Capital at a rate based on SOFR, a floating dollar benchmark. The service is local; that tranche of the debt is not. A conversion would match the two.

Suriname did not borrow less. It now owes in the currency it earns, and the same option is already written into other nations’ contracts.
