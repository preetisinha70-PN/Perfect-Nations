The Living Line, and how it is measured

The Living LineThe poverty line measures income. The Living Line measures a life: food, water, shelter, energy, education, health and livelihood. Perfect Capita is the yearly cost, per person, of lifting everyone above the Living Line in all seven basic living standards. Coverage scores each standard strictly out of 100, and a nation is held at its weakest: Barbados at 69, by food, with health at 82 and livelihood at 82.5 next.

Perfect Nations estimate in 2025 US dollars, to be confirmed with Barbados. Perfect Capita adds the national poverty lines (Survey of Living Conditions 2016, carried to 2025 prices) to public spending per person on education and health, so nothing is counted twice. The Gap applies FAO’s 2022 food insecurity figure to a population of 282,623. The strict measures

The strict measures. Food: share not moderately or severely food insecure (FAO). Water: safely managed drinking water where measured; where it is not, basic water, marked as such. Shelter: the lower of safely managed sanitation and the share outside urban slums. Energy: the lower of electricity per person against 1,000 kWh and clean cooking. Education: the lower of children reading by the end of primary school and lower-secondary completion. Health: WHO service coverage index. Livelihood: share above the stricter of the national poverty line and the World Bank line for the nation’s income group. Each figure links to its source.

  • GovernmentAdvocacyThe Bridgetown InitiativeConvened July 2022 · Version 3.0, September 2024 · Ongoing$650B

    New SDR issuance sought under Bridgetown 3.0

    The Bridgetown Initiative began as a high-level retreat convened in Bridgetown in July 2022, went to the UN General Assembly that September and to COP27 in November. Version 2.0 followed in April 2023 and fed the Paris Summit’s Paris Pact for People and Planet in June 2023 and COP28’s climate finance framework that December. Version 3.0 was released in September 2024 around three pillars: change the rules of the game, shock-proof economies, and dramatically increase financing. It puts the annual climate and nature requirement for emerging and developing economies at $1.8 trillion and the SDG requirement at $1.2 trillion, of which $850 billion must come from external sources. It asks for $500 billion a year in additional official lending, $300 billion a year of thirty to fifty year MDB financing, a $650 billion new SDR allocation, and a minimum $120 billion IDA21 replenishment, alongside levies on aviation, shipping, fossil fuel windfalls and extreme wealth.

Its natural disaster clause allows Barbados to capitalise interest and defer principal for two years after a qualifying earthquake, tropical cyclone or rainfall event — the drafting the rest of the market has since borrowed.
